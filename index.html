How it runs

One brief in. Sixteen things happening at once.

Most studios run these steps in a queue: research waits on the brief, design waits on research, code waits on design. We don't. The only two places anything waits are the rows in amber.

run · client-marketing-site · week 1 Running

The amber rows are people. A senior designer picks the direction and throws out whatever looks generated. A senior engineer reads the code before it merges. Nothing reaches your client without both, because a pipeline that approves its own work is just a faster way to ship something wrong.

Before you ask

The four things that actually blow up a project.

None of them are design problems. Here is how each one is handled, in writing, before it happens.

01

The client changes their mind in week three.

They usually do. Two rounds of revision are already in the price. Past that we re-quote the difference before touching anything, so the surprise arrives on a quote instead of an invoice.

02

The copy never arrives.

Nine times out of ten the text is the long pole, and it is never the designer's fault. We build against realistic placeholder copy and swap the real thing in one pass, so a late Word document doesn't move the launch date.

03

Their IT department says no to the stack.

Ask us on the first call, before you quote your client. If it has to run on their WordPress or their internal framework, sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes it is an honest no, which is still cheaper than finding out in week four.

04

Feedback comes back as forty comments and no decision.

We ask for one person with the final say and one place to leave notes. If your client can't give you that, say so at the start and we will build the review rounds around it instead of pretending.

FAQ

Questions we get every week.

Will my client know you exist?

No. We work under your name. We can use an address on your domain if you want one.

Do you tell my client AI was involved?

That is your call and it goes in the contract. We follow whatever you commit to, including a written statement of what was generated and what was authored if your client asks for one.

Who actually does the work?

A small senior team directing the pipeline. The same people from brief to handover. No junior hand-off halfway through, no rotating roster.

What if you disappear halfway through?

Everything lives in your accounts from day one: your Figma, your repo, your Vercel. If we vanished tomorrow you would still have a working project and a developer could pick it up on Monday. That is deliberate, and it is worth checking with anyone else you outsource to.

Can you work inside our existing codebase?

Often yes. Send us the repo before you quote your client, not after. If it is in bad shape we will say so and tell you what it costs to work in it anyway.

Who owns the code and the files?

You do, in full, once the final invoice is paid — Figma, repo, deployment, domain. We keep no licence and reuse nothing from your project.

Will my own developers be able to maintain it?

That is the test we hold it to. Ordinary Next.js and React, typed, commented where it isn't obvious, with a written handover. If your developer can't take it over, we didn't finish.

What if we don't like the first round?

Two rounds of revision are in every quote. If the direction is wrong after round one, that is on us and we redo it at our cost.

Can we take design only, or build only?

Either. Most take both, because the handover between the two is where projects usually lose a week.

What stack do you build in?

Next.js, React, Tailwind, Supabase, Vercel. Ask about anything else — the answer is sometimes yes.
